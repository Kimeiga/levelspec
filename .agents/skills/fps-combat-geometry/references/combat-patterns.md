# Combat geometry: mechanisms and counterexamples

Inspect only the feature families relevant to the requested change. Test relations
in the real movement/visibility system rather than treating examples as dimensions.

## Shape the encounter through architecture

For a new layout or broad LOS repair, compare changes to circulation and enclosure
before adding standalone obstacles. A bridge can bend around a building; doorways
can be offset; a narrow connector can wind into a wider preparation room; a room
can have a meaningful recess, angled edge or projecting service core. These shapes
should establish when threats appear and which movements expose the player.

Judge the whole composition with those decisions: building masses, room identity,
route hierarchy and silhouettes should read as a coherent place. A collection of
rectangular arenas or a straight crossing patched with repetitive boxes can conceal
an unresolved layout. Retain simple rooms and deliberate long lanes where they
support the brief. Making every passage tight, irregular or winding can obstruct
passing, trades, utility and quiet regrouping without creating useful strategy.

Open courts and arenas with purposeful freestanding cover can also be coherent.
Central machinery, low obstacles or landmark props can offer cover and orientation
while adding visual character. Check their approach choices, support, counterplay
and readability; neither open space nor a prop in its center is itself a defect.

## Hallways, bends and thresholds

Evaluate approach → preparation → first contact → follow-up → fallback. Width
affects lateral position choice, passing and simultaneous entry; a bend controls
when new threats appear; a doorway can constrain the second player's sightline even
when two bodies technically fit. Check whether obstacle collision traps the follower
or creates an unintended narrow firing gap.

Compare which threats become visible together. A sloped corner or carefully placed
blocker can separate a difficult two-sided check. Conversely, intentional mutually
supporting defenders can justify a coordinated flash, smoke or second approach.

**Counterexamples:** wider is not always better: it can prevent a smoke from covering
the entrance or remove useful defensive resistance. Widening a doorway does not fix
the incentive to remain outside if advancing still offers little benefit. A narrow
corridor can be useful when its commitment is deliberate and there is feasible
response; it fails when the only plausible sequence is unavoidable serial deaths.

## Crates, recesses and cover spacing

For each relevant object, identify the approach directions it protects from and the
directions it exposes. A freestanding crate permits switching sides or circling;
directional cover may favor one team's approach. Trace how ownership changes during
a take, hold or retake. Moving the same crate across a corner can change both the
attacker's clearing burden and the defender's distance to safety.

Height determines available over-top and side peeks. Inspect standing and crouching
views with actual camera/body geometry; eye-height cover may produce a tiny target,
while a visual gap can reveal feet or a weapon. Check collision separately. Material
and thickness influence penetration only if the target engine implements it.

Gaps between objects set exposed travel time. Compare that exposure to a plausible
window created by support, suppression, utility or enemy movement. A protected pocket
can provide time to observe and prepare utility without contributing another direct
firing angle.

Assess preparation, exposed movement and repositioning across active encounters.
Where low cover serves those needs, develop its sequence: a bridge encounter might
benefit from a refuge after entry, a place to duck or prepare, and a repositioning
option when the opposite entrance is threatened. Bends, tall blockers and low cover
can address different exposures; choose the combination for the encounter and
mechanics. Check the full approach and following player's view; cover that stops
the entry safely can still trap or screen the teammate.

Integrate low refuges into the setting where useful: benches, planters, machinery,
retaining edges and service islands can provide ducking and preparation positions.
Give them the same dimensions, IDs and two-sided exposure checks as freestanding
crates. Their composition and spacing should explain a movement sequence without
turning the route into an obstacle maze. Assess whether the architecture already
provides useful preparation and reset positions, and what a low refuge would add;
do not require a fixture or uniform cover recipe for every active route.

Choose low-cover dimensions from the actual posture and camera contract. Inspect
standing, low-eye and elevated reverse views for over-top exposure, feet, weapon
protrusion and small gaps. A lowered inspection camera establishes only visibility;
verify supported crouching and the body in the intended engine. Low cover must
offer usable protection without turning every gap into a tiny-target hold.

**Counterexamples:** safe preparation space can be valuable without charging a
penalty at every location. A hidden recess without escape can be a strong committed
ambush rather than a defect. More crates can create redundant hiding checks, movement
snags and overwhelming angles. Removing cover can restore meaningful commitment.

Recheck low cover when nearby ground changes height. Its base/top relative to each
observer and target matters more than the object's nominal height alone; a raised
attacker may see a body that the same obstacle concealed in a level duel.

## Vantage points, grades and elevation

Evaluate what the perch sees, how much body it exposes, how it is reached, what
support it needs and how the occupant can leave. Check the approach continuously:
ramps can expose an approaching player's body before the elevated player becomes
visible. Compare side approaches and other feasible tools for contesting the angle.

Choose elevation at a useful scale. Compare flat ground with a sustained grade
through a corridor or broad section, local steps/crests, raised pockets and stacked
routes. Tie the choice to visibility or movement. A crest can change when eyes,
head and body appear; a long incline can change an entire objective approach.
These are hypotheses to inspect, not reasons to place local bumps everywhere.

Trace standing and ascending eye/body exposure continuously across the transition,
then inspect the reverse view. Check whether a teammate behind or below can see
the defender, whether cover still protects at the new elevation, and whether the
occupant can retreat or change level after being located. Include landing depth,
head/shoulder clearance, support at both ends and the actual climb/drop contract.
A lower lane below an elevated hold may conceal the entry's feet while exposing
their head, or make the follower unable to trade; an overview can hide these costs.

For long grades, inspect intermediate positions across the entire slope, not just
its endpoints. Verify changing relative cover/eye heights, actual base contact,
headroom, transitions, support and uphill/downhill reversibility. A flat section
or an entirely flat map can be the appropriate choice; no elevation quota applies.

Inspect whether the same doorway presents high and low threats simultaneously.
Deliberate multi-level teamwork can be useful, but uncontrolled vertical checks
can make an approach unreadable. Compare retaining, relocating, lowering or removing
the height change. Reassess after plant/capture or other ownership changes; do not
assume the useful entrance geometry also supports a feasible retake. The
[elevation research revisit](../../../../docs/research/fps-elevation-and-visibility.md)
records original developer accounts and their limits.

Optional skill jumps can offer situational shortcuts while preserving usable
ordinary routes where the brief requires them. Distinguish an intended teammate
boost from a jump assumed possible because two surfaces look close.

**Counterexamples:** height alone is neither advantage nor flaw. A perch can control
a long approach while losing a close fight or requiring a costly exit. Adding a
flank that arrives after the objective resolves does not meaningfully contest it.
Without current mechanics, do not transfer historical head-peek or crouch exploits.

Include height-aware visibility in the layout stage. Ground-height walls can leave
an elevated route with views into every adjacent region. Tall buildings and
occluders can constrain those views; preserve intended doorways, crossing bands,
soffits and landings when adding them. Repeat from actual compiled eye positions,
including along an ascending ramp, before accepting the relationship.

## Team support and utility

Inspect the first and following player's sightlines, not just their separation.
Can the follower punish the entry's killer before retreat/recovery? Can two defenders
cover one approach from different directions, and what do they leave unguarded?
Can a support player throw a useful flash without exposing themselves, and can they
join afterward? Treat these as relationships between spaces.

Smoke may conceal a crossing without blocking physical access; a grenade may deny
a recess but consume resources needed later. Counterplay can require allocating a
player's attention, gaining neighboring territory, changing timing or threatening
another objective rather than adding a local escape to every position.

**Counterexamples:** universally easy angle isolation can remove the need for
teamwork. Universally safe retreat can make strong holds costless. A crossfire is not
unbeatable simply because two firing lines overlap on the drawing.

## Review note example

Use the project's existing drawing/inventory format. A compact entry can read:

> C-07, freestanding crate at north threshold. Intended to screen the west window
> while attackers stage; it remains exposed to the upper landing. Moving it toward
> the threshold may let an entry stop safely but obstruct the following player's
> trade. Compare both positions from the west window, landing and follower pose.
> Prediction only; body passage and live trade timing have not been verified.

This records a consequence and evidence limit without inventing a universal spacing
rule or claiming simulated behavior.

## Selected evidence

Read the repository's [research annotations](../../../../docs/research/fps-strategy-and-combat-geometry.md)
when a source or comparison is useful; loading the complete corpus is unnecessary.

- [Yoder: Judge A Map By Its Cover](https://andrewyoderdesign.blog/2022/04/14/a-few-kinds-of-cover/): original Dust II design analysis of directional/freestanding cover and corner relationships. Casual-player/designer perspective, not professional balance proof.
- [Riot: Split 2.01](https://playvalorant.com/en-us/news/game-updates/valorant-patch-notes-2-01/): developer explanations of doorway width, utility staging and separating simultaneous checks.
- [Riot: Icebox 4.04](https://playvalorant.com/en-us/news/game-updates/deep-dive-into-icebox-4-04-changes/): entry support, overly comfortable cover, stray sightlines and feet-only exposure.
- [Dignitas: T-Side Isolation](https://dignitas.gg/articles/t-side-isolation-how-it-applies-to-clutches-and-entry-fragging): historical tactical explanation distinguishes movement-based clearing from utility-assisted isolation. Do not transfer its numeric timers or simplified peeking terminology as current facts.

These document mechanisms, observations or intent. They do not establish present-day
dimensions or guarantee that a proposed edit improves the target map.
