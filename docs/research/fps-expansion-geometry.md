# Additional geometry sources: 18 distinct works

Research date: 2026-11-08. These sources are additional to `fps-strategy-and-combat-geometry.md`. Mirrors, author homepages, and duplicate presentations of the same work are not counted. Read means source text inspected; a located video means only its creator/event description was inspected. These are practitioner analyses and design explanations, not controlled proof of current CS2 outcomes.

1. **Andrew Yoder — [The Door Problem of Combat Design](https://andrewyoderdesign.blog/2019/08/04/the-door-problem-of-combat-design/), 2019. Read.** A player retreats to a doorway because it reduces attack directions; making the door wider does not necessarily change that incentive. The analysis treats useful territory as developing one's options while limiting the opponent's. **Mechanism:** geometry changes the comparative cost of committing versus staying back. **Limit:** single-player Quake-style encounters; enemies and resources differ from competitive CS. Strong general framework, not a command to eliminate defensive door play.

2. **Ben Bauer — [Level Design Cover 101: Placement & Mindset](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/level-design-cover-101---placement-mindset), 2021. Creator article read; embedded video located, not watched.** The creator explicitly examines cover arrangement from simple corners to convex and concave spaces. **Viewing question:** how does moving or rotating a cover object alter the set of approaches a player can take? **Limit:** the short accompanying article establishes scope, not the particular conclusions of every video example.

3. **Niels Tiercelin — [Practical Guide on First Person Level Design](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/practical-guide-on-first-person-level-design), 2017. Read.** Uses Mirror's Edge and Bad Company 2 examples. A four-route traversal can be presented as successive binary choices; movement mechanics and choke structure determine which routes have meaning. **Mechanism:** choices can be spatially staged rather than simultaneously overwhelming. **Limit:** several sweeping claims and recommendations are personal opinion; do not reuse its old map-balance statements as current facts.

4. **Curtis Gaunt — [Analyzing Level Layouts to Improve Level Design in Competitive FPS](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/analyzing-level-layouts-to-improve-level-design-in-competitive-fps), 2017. Read.** Compares Dust II, Overwatch's Temple of Anubis, and Call of Duty: Ghosts' Sovereign through routes, objectives, verticality, and changing spawn systems. **Mechanism:** the consequences of a lane or choke depend on objective activation and movement abilities. **Limit:** coarse diagrams and categorical entrance-count claims oversimplify the actual maps. Useful comparative exercise, not reliable universal lane-count law.

5. **Andrew Yoder — [Playgrounds & Level Design](https://andrewyoderdesign.blog/2019/03/25/playgrounds-level-design/), 2019. Read.** Connects vantage points, routes, skill checks, loops, and recovery. Advanced Quake movement can offer shortcuts without being the only path. **Mechanism:** the same structure supports different routes according to skill and intent. **Limit:** an exploratory framework for broad play, including social play; reducing failure cost is not always compatible with competitive round stakes.

6. **Andrew Yoder — [Halo and Inflexible PvP](https://andrewyoderdesign.blog/2017/12/28/inflexible-pvp/), 2017. Read.** Derelict combines upper-floor powerups, exposed teleport access, and allied respawning to reinforce top-floor control. Longest instead permits spawn swaps that reset pressure. **Mechanism:** geometry, access costs, items, and respawns can jointly turn a nominal deathmatch into holding one dominant territory. **Limit:** qualitative account of particular matches and historical Halo behavior, not a large-sample study.

7. **Andrew Yoder — [Halo's Multiplayer Maps and Public Parks](https://andrewyoderdesign.blog/2017/12/09/halos-multiplayer-maps-and-public-parks/), 2017. Read.** Analyzes movement, shields, resources, and map structures as a shared play environment. Halo's slow acceleration and floaty committed jumps make positional mistakes costly. **Mechanism:** a gap or exposed route must be evaluated using actual movement and recovery mechanics. **Limit:** experiential design analysis; much of the broader argument concerns social needs rather than competitive balance.

8. **Andrew Yoder — [The Red House of Sainte Marie du Mont](https://andrewyoderdesign.blog/2018/01/14/the-red-house-of-sainte-marie-du-mont/), 2018. Read.** A conspicuous central two-story building offers overlooks but can be bypassed and punished with grenades. Its perceived importance differs from the value of more advanced street positions. **Mechanism:** architecture can invite occupation without making it optimal; alternative lanes and tight interiors create counterplay. **Limit:** explicitly based on the author's Hardcore Team Deathmatch experience, not all modes or players.

9. **Iuliu-Cosmin Oniscu — [The Misunderstood Crate: Why Modern FPS Games Get Cover Wrong](https://iuliu-cosmin-oniscu.medium.com/cover-safety-intel-planning-c9cbbd0ce078), 2020. Read.** Examines cover as a break in visibility that provides time to assess and plan, rather than merely an obstacle behind which to stop. **Mechanism:** cover shapes a decision interval within the combat rhythm. **Limit:** polemical practitioner framing spanning FPS, stealth, and cover shooters; its taxonomy is not an empirical universal.

10. **Iuliu-Cosmin Oniscu — [Articulating a Visual Language System in Level Design](https://iuliu-cosmin-oniscu.medium.com/how-to-articulate-a-visual-language-system-in-a-level-design-environment-17fecbcbede9), 2020. Read.** Connects character abilities, repeatable metric kits, consistent interactions, and readable environmental opportunities. **Mechanism:** players can make intentional choices when shapes reliably communicate actions. **Limit:** example dimensions are game-specific assumptions, not universal player or cover measurements.

11. **Iuliu-Cosmin Oniscu — [The Dual Function of Cover: Guiding Player Action in Stealth and Combat Layouts](https://iuliu-cosmin-oniscu.medium.com/how-to-handle-cover-placement-d10580faac66), 2020. Read.** Relates distance between cover to windows in which NPCs look away or stop shooting. Also uses architectural niches to integrate cover without losing directional clarity. **Mechanism:** exposed transit time interacts with the duration of an opportunity. **Limit:** fixed AI behavior and shooting intervals do not transfer directly to human opponents; in PvP those windows must be created, inferred, or contested.

12. **The Level Design Book contributors — [Cover](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/combat/cover). Read.** Distinguishes movement around cover from movement with cover and discusses height, width, solidity, facing, and consistent affordances. **Mechanism:** cover behavior depends on the surrounding movement and damage systems. **Limit:** several Dust II examples derive from Yoder; those repetitions are not independent corroboration. Retain its original taxonomy and cross-game comparison as the contribution.

13. **The Level Design Book contributors — [Encounter](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/combat/encounter). Read.** Frames encounters as systemic challenges supporting varied tactics; examines initial surveying, entry footholds, battle progression, and outcomes. **Mechanism:** initial information and a workable position can allow players to form and revise plans. **Limit:** principally PvE; much of the foothold section builds on Yoder, so do not count that as new independent evidence.

14. **The Level Design Book contributors — [Flow](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/layout/flow). Read.** Dust doors demonstrate that interrupted movement and dangerous turns can serve strategy even when they would count as poor smooth traversal. The chapter argues for restrained, mechanic-dependent verticality. **Mechanism:** friction can create a deliberate decision or commitment. **Limit:** supports a contextual design question, not an objective measurement of fun or a rule that every corridor needs friction.

15. **The Level Design Book contributors — [Metrics](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/metrics). Read.** Separates measured player physics from designer-chosen building guidelines and explicitly rejects the idea that correct dimensions guarantee a good experience. **Mechanism:** widths, heights, and distances need an intended behavior and play context. **Limit:** methodology rather than a particular map case; use for evaluation, not as evidence that a dimension creates strategic depth.

16. **Michael Barclay, Sam Howels, Pete Ellis — [Creating Conflict: Combat Design for AAA Action Games](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023860/Creating-Conflict-Combat-Design-for), GDC Europe 2016. Official abstract read; video located, not watched.** The abstract explicitly includes specific geometric arrangements of cover in first/third-person, linear/open-world encounters. **Viewing question:** which arrangements change threat fronts, challenge, and available movement? **Limit:** no detailed presentation claims verified from the video; do not cite its mere existence as proof of a particular principle.

17. **Pascal Luban — [Multiplayer Level Design In-Depth, Part 1: The Specific Constraints of Multiplayer Level Design](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/multiplayer-level-design-in-depth-part-1-the-specific-constraints-of-multiplayer-level-design), 2006. Read.** Original multiplayer designer explains an Aquarius ventilation grille: defensive grenade use can destroy it and unintentionally open a quicker attacker route. Also discusses players selecting efficient tactics under repeated play. **Mechanism:** one local action can change future access and create a tactical cost beyond immediate damage. **Limit:** dated technical constraints should not be adopted as modern networking guidance.

18. **Emilia Schatz — [Defining Environment Language for Video Games](https://80.lv/articles/defining-environment-language-for-video-games/), 2017. First-person designer explanation read.** Describes preserving blockmesh intent through final art with deliberate shape language and consistent affordances. **Mechanism:** visual treatment must communicate what geometry lets the player do, otherwise plausible-looking cover or traversal produces mistaken plans. **Limit:** primarily Naughty Dog single-player production practice; example height bands are not CS2 standards.

## Two candidate principles to test

### A. Give the player a situational advantage, priced by a consequential commitment

Working synthesis: an interesting position changes what a player can do, but reaching, using, or leaving it commits something the opponent can contest: exposure, time, information, mobility, resources, or teammate attention. The costs need not be equal or immediate. Examine the position together with its approaches, exits, and surrounding mechanics.

Supporting cases include the red house's overlooks versus bypasses/grenades, Halo's committed jump routes, cover-to-cover timing windows, and the destructible grille's future access consequence.

Counterexample: imposing a cost does not guarantee a choice. Derelict's upper floor remains attractive because its benefits and reinforcement mechanisms dominate the alternatives. Conversely, a temporary safe staging space may be useful precisely because it is safe; evaluate the subsequent transition and whole encounter before insisting every square meter carry a penalty.

### B. Design transitions that let players revise a plan, then make commitment legible

Working synthesis: interesting geometry alternates opportunities to assess/reposition with transitions that expose or constrain a chosen action. A corridor bend, cover edge, doorway, or jump can form such a transition. Readability lets players reason about its consequences; multiple credible follow-ups prevent the threshold from being a rote execution.

Supporting cases include Yoder's doorway comparison, Oniscu's planning intervals, Dust doors' deliberate movement friction, and Quake playground loops/optional shortcuts.

Counterexample: too much comfort at the entrance can make every internal option irrelevant; adding a wider doorway alone leaves the same incentive. Too many disjointed thresholds can instead become busywork. The design test is whether changing circumstances change the sensible action, not the raw number of corners, routes, or props.
