# Elevation as a tactical relationship

Research revisit: 2026-11-08. This rechecked the [99-work index](fps-source-index.md) and all five supporting annotation/synthesis files for elevation, stairs, ramps, perches, exposure, support and counterplay. It does not mean99 complete remote publications were reread or99 independent experiments exist. Selected original designer accounts and primary coaching explanations were reopened below; no video was watched during this revisit. Historical examples describe mechanisms and intent, not current CS2 coordinates, movement constants or measured balance.

## Principle to carry into the skills

**Use local elevation changes to change what players can discover, expose, contest and do next.** Specify the relationship a stair, ramp, crest, low pocket or raised apron creates; compare it with the flat alternative. Combine these changes with low cover, substantial occluding architecture, access costs and feasible support/counterplay. A flat ground plane plus one separate bridge misses many useful local relationships. Random height changes everywhere are not a substitute for purpose.

This is a design synthesis, not a finding that more elevation always improves a map. The existing source library supports adding some height changes and removing others according to the fight they produce.

The broader corpus adds supporting context: Nuke/Overpass tactical guides relate connected levels and routes to information/rotations; Yoder's red-house study separates an attractive overlook from optimal territory; Ubisoft reworks change stairs together with adjacent connectors. Their original annotations and limitations remain in the library. None establishes that every encounter needs a high position.

## Concrete mechanisms to design and test

1. **Visibility changes during approach.** A rise can conceal or reveal different parts of the approaching body before either eye sees the opponent. A crest can temporarily separate near and far territory. Record who sees feet, torso, head and muzzle throughout movement; inspect ascending and descending, and compare a lateral-peek approach. Treat desirable concealment and accidental one-way body exposure separately.
2. **A choice of peek height and side.** A shallow landing or raised apron can open a different angle over low cover, while the lower route preserves concealment or a lateral escape. The player should be choosing a consequence, not merely climbing decorative steps. Check whether a lower opponent can contest it from another position, with support or by threatening elsewhere.
3. **Cover changes with relative height.** A1.1m obstacle measured from local feet may allow ducking and standing exposure on that surface. The same obstacle viewed from a raised attacker may protect much less. Give every object a base elevation and top elevation; test both observer levels. Raising the ground without reconsidering cover can silently erase its role.
4. **Support and commitment.** A stair or ramp can separate an entry from the follower, block the follower's trade view, expose an ascent or delay a retreat. Compare the elevated player's advantage with the cost of access, attention, teammate allocation and onward travel. A dramatic perch with an unusably late counter-route has no meaningful counterplay from that route.
5. **Local variation versus stacked overload.** Shallow level changes can create multiple nearby positions without a full second floor. Full stacked routes add vertical attention, sound/radar ambiguity and possible dominance. Keep ground, stairs, landing, underside and upper route readable as distinct relationships, including where they do not connect.

## Spillway studies to compare before authoring

These are hypotheses for a labeled plan/section and review, not approved dimensions or model changes:

- **A approach shoulder:** compare flat forecourt with a shallow raised shoulder reached by ordinary stairs and an alternate ground approach. Predict a useful gate/cover peek while retaining exposure to rear pressure. Check whether the shoulder can also see unrelated routes; substantial neighboring architecture may need to bound its view.
- **Mid approach crest or shallow change:** compare the current flat transition with a modest rise/fall that changes when the next threat becomes visible. Preserve staging and low-cover choices. Trace both bodies across the crest; reject an accidental feet-only or head-only shot rather than calling every hidden approach strategic.
- **B service apron:** compare a small raised holding/repositioning area with the ground plant route and a broad ordinary ramp. Predict one local view over cover with a commitment or flank cost. Inspect retake ownership reversal, follower support and whether the approach can be stopped from one universally superior post.

Evaluate one or a few consequential relationships first. The map does not need a height change every meter. Deliberate long lanes remain valid candidates when their access, cover and counterplay justify them; elevation should not restore an unintended spawn view or panoramic bridge perch.

## AI review contract

Before3D, annotate local ground/landing elevations, stairs/ramp endpoints, cover bases/tops, tall occluders and observer/target eye offsets. Draw sections through the actual fight, not only a top-down route graph. State the intended action, opponent response, teammate contribution and next decision. Compare a flat alternative when the benefit is unclear.

After compilation, revisit the same locations in XYZ: paired viewpoints, several positions along each transition, standing/low-eye studies, supported-body movement and changed low-cover relationships. Extend visibility scans to actual new floor heights; sharedXY must retain separateZ samples. A low camera is not crouch/weapon simulation, navigation is not continuous collision proof, and optical ray success is not a measured combat advantage. Leave movement, sound, utility and human adaptation claims for the intended engine/playtests.
