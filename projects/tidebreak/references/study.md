## Three deliberately contrasting organizations

These are review proposals, not a favorite layout or generator template. Compare them against the generated relationships before choosing or combining anything. No user locks prescribe a dock count, room count, route count, objective arrangement or symmetry.

### S1 — One working hall, two occluding hulls

Interpret a cluster of graph nodes as territories inside one large, roofed ship-repair shed. Two hulls are offset in both length and lateral position. The shorter hull leaves an end apron that crosses the shed; the longer one divides the central work lane from a side repair platform. A broad, partial mezzanine wraps only the landward apse and one side, with two separated ordinary ascents. A small service yard outside the open end continues toward a separate workshop mass.

Before/after opportunity: a graph with three approaches to a key node would normally become three halls entering a room. Here one approach arrives along a curved hull shoulder, another emerges underneath the partial mezzanine, and a third reaches the upper apron. They share a recognizable volume but offer different information and commitment. A player can threaten around the bow and then reverse toward the stern; a teammate on the mezzanine can cover only the crossing apron, not chase every target. An opponent can concede the center and hold the outer hull curve or pressure the workshop so the gallery player must leave.

Cost/risk: one shared volume can accidentally grant too many transfers, a hull-tip angle can become a dominant crossfire, and the mezzanine may turn into a panoramic perch. Accept fewer graph-independent routes if that produces a stronger front. Test whether at least two genuinely different post-contact plans survive the actual occluders; trace which transfers really exist around both hulls. Local experiments: offset a hull by several meters, interrupt one mezzanine span, widen one repair pocket for a follower. Do not make every bay reachable above and below.

### S2 — A yard wrapped around a sunken basin

Begin with continuous grade-level service ground and subtract a rounded dock void. Put a coherent covered slip along one side and a pump/engineering house at the closed head, leaving an outdoor route around the opposite rim. The dock bottom is an occupied destination and conditional shortcut, with a partial hull/cradle mass interrupting its axis. Two sets of stairs are carved into different retaining-wall sectors; their intermediate ledges continue around only part of the basin. One crossing near the head can become a bridge with actual support and clearance; it need not connect every level.

Before/after opportunity: opponents controlling the rim can see some lower movement but cannot immediately follow it. A lower team can bypass a contested yard sector, emerge through the offset stair recess, or commit to the bottom objective; a supporting player on the nearer ledge covers the next landing rather than the whole basin. The other side can contest the far exit or switch pressure to the shed, forcing low players to choose a slower retreat or a risky ascent.

Cost/risk: low territory may be a death bowl, two entrances may share one physical landing, and the bridge can shorten every rotation excessively. This organization intentionally trades some free movement for readable commitment. Test opposing standing and follower views at each landing, and compare low-path travel against the exterior rim in both directions. Local experiments: curve a retaining-wall return to hide the lower stair, preserve one exposed transit stretch, place the hull so its bow gives a temporary reset without blocking the onward route.

### S3 — Service peninsula between dock fingers

Use two long negative spaces to form an irregular service peninsula. A covered repair shed and a pump/service structure occupy its interior at different alignments; circulation runs through their open industrial thresholds and around their sides. The peninsula head is reachable by the long service road and a short but exposed caisson-side crossing. A second territory sits inland around the dock heads, so controlling the narrow crossing does not automatically control every destination. A single lower maintenance route can connect one recess to a different yard sector; special movement, if enabled, remains optional rather than certifying the map's only escape.

Before/after opportunity: instead of a compact graph where every additional edge accelerates contact, the voids preserve physically meaningful separation. A team can demonstrate pressure at the exposed crossing while moving inland around a dock head; the defender sees partial evidence but cannot rotate instantly through the water. Holding the service peninsula grants useful information and an attack origin, but relinquishes inland coverage. Teammates can split attention between the shed threshold and the dock-head road; opponents can isolate the peninsula by contesting its landward access rather than requiring a new flank door into its rear.

Cost/risk: the tip can become an inert dead end, the caisson crossing can be a single compulsory choke, or the long wrap can become empty downtime. The architecture needs developed intermediate spaces—repair apron, service arch, pump court and a curved dock-head turn—rather than simply stretching two paths. Test the choice after the first contact: can a player retreat, switch pressure or coordinate a timed push using actual distances? Local experiments: a partial through-shed passage offers information before commitment; a broken sightline at the dock head allows a group to re-form; remove an unnecessary bridge if it erases the strategic difference between the two sides.

## Implications for the prototype's second and third passes

- A node should be allowed to mean an apron, landing, portion of one shared hall, or exterior territory. One node per enclosed room would recreate the original anchoring problem.
- Realization can choose positive mass first (hulls/buildings), negative space first (basins), or an envelope first (shared shed). Those change relationships more substantially than changing polygons on otherwise identical rooms.
- Realized connections must be reconciled after grouping spaces. An open hall creates additional transfers; a shared stair recess can collapse apparently independent edges. A bridge needs actual access, headroom and a usable departure.
- Curves should come from hulls, basin heads and industrial turning areas; regular truss bays and service buildings can stay rectilinear. Deforming every wall would weaken coherence.
- Keep the first comparison broad: S1 concentrates encounters in a shared volume; S2 makes a nested low/high territory; S3 uses voids and commitment to separate plans. Do not immediately force all three into one layout.
- Ordinary walking ramps/stairs can establish the core trial. Ladders, jumps and crawl routes are later configurable experiments with separate engine evidence.
- A rough generated interpretation is useful even when it is awkward. The AI should retain the interesting relationship, record why it changes or discards parts of the graph, and then develop coherent architecture. Neither the seed graph nor these three proposals is a lock.
